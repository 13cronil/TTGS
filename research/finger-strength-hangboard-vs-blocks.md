# Finger-Strength Training for the Hybrid Climber-Lifter: Hangboards vs. Lifting Pins/Blocks — A Physiotherapist's Evidence Review

## TL;DR
- **Keep doing both.** For an experienced hybrid climber/lifter who treats connective-tissue longevity as a first-class goal, the best-supported approach is exactly what you're running: high-intensity hangboard work (Max Hangs/MED) for peak recruitment plus block/pinch pulls for load-precise, joint-controllable, thumb-specific and back-3 loading. Gilmore, Klimek, Abrahamsson & Baar (*Sports Medicine – Open* 2024, 10:125) concluded that "the increase in strength with low-intensity long duration holds is equivalent to that of maximal hang training" and that "the two types of training have an additive effect on grip strength" — combining modalities is evidence-supported, not redundant.
- **Total tissue load is the real variable, not session count.** With 3 climbing + 3 lifting days, your fingers are loaded most days; literature and coaching consensus converge on **2 (occasionally 3) genuinely hard finger sessions per week with ≥48h between them**, counting limit bouldering as a finger session. Manage the weekly sum, not each modality in isolation.
- **Your hypermobility should bias you toward block pulls, an open-hand/half-crimp emphasis, slower progression, and muscular co-contraction/joint-stability work.** Lax tissue *does* adapt to progressive loading (stiffness can increase), but you need longer ramp-ups, tighter joint-position control (avoid DIP hyperextension/full crimp at volume), and may benefit from the Baar collagen-loading protocol.

## Key Findings

**1. The evidence base is strongest for high-intensity hangboarding, but is surprisingly favorable to low-intensity and no-hang alternatives.** Eva López's controlled work (López-Rivera & González-Badillo) established that Max Hangs — especially 4 weeks of Maximal Added Weight (MAW) on an 18mm edge followed by 4 weeks of Minimum Edge Depth (MED) — produce the largest maximal-strength gains, with Eric Hörst's summary of the data reporting "average strength gain of 15.2% after 4 weeks and 28% after 8 weeks." Their later trial (López-Rivera & González-Badillo, 2019, *Journal of Human Kinetics* 66:183–195; 26 climbers, mean 7c+/8a) found intermittent hangs (repeaters) superior for endurance — grip endurance improved ~45% at 8 weeks for IntHangs vs 34.1% for MaxHangs. The 2024 Gilmore/Baar study — a large retrospective of Crimpd-app users — found low-intensity "Abrahangs" improved grip strength as much as Max Hangs, and combining them was additive. [upi](https://oalib-perpustakaan.upi.edu/Record/doaj_c74da81d84d54ec0b07a29368544b687/Description?print=1) Effect sizes were modest (Cohen's *d* ≈ 0.29 Abrahangs, 0.45 Max Hangs; combined group ~5.8% strength-to-weight gain vs ~2.5–3.2% for either alone) and the design was retrospective with self-selected, unverified loads.

**2. Block pulls/lifting pins load the finger differently and are increasingly favored by Lattice's own coaches.** Per Lattice Training's own blog ("How to Train Pick-Ups with a Portable Hangboard"): "Over the past 2 years, the Lattice coaches have replaced fingerboard workouts with lifting edge workouts in over 30% of training plans." Block pulls isolate the finger flexors with fine, sub-bodyweight load control, remove shoulder/body-tension demands, allow grip resets between reps, and are markedly better for pinch (they force true thumb engagement, whereas hanging from a hangboard pinch lets you cheat with opposing pressure). The tradeoff: they are less specific to the overhead, whole-chain loading of actual climbing, and add instability that can slightly reduce force output.

**3. Loading mechanics and injury profile differ meaningfully between modalities.** In the full crimp, the A2 pulley bears the highest tension: Miro, vanSonnenberg, Sabb & Schöffl (2021, *Wilderness & Environmental Medicine*) report "forces 3 to 4 times greater than at the distal phalanges… Recreational rock climbers can load the A2 pulley with forces between 380 N and 700 N, which equates to an average of 54 kg." Pulley injuries account for 15–20% of all climbing injuries. Hangboarding on small edges under full bodyweight (or one-arm) delivers the highest, least-controllable peak loads and the most swing/shear; block pulls let you titrate load precisely and reset grip, which is why they're the standard tool for rehab and for training loads far from bodyweight (e.g., a 6mm two-finger pocket). Both are isometric (yielding) contractions; overcoming-isometric "recruitment pulls" (Tyler Nelson) and density hangs add distinct stimuli.

**4. Frequency is capped by tissue recovery, not muscular recovery.** Consensus across López (48h recovery from Max Hangs), Eric Hörst (no more than 4 aggregate hard finger/climbing days/week; ≥3 rest days), and Tyler Nelson (2/week beginners, 3/week experts, same-day-as-climbing acceptable if separated 4–6h) [Camp4humanperformance](https://www.camp4humanperformance.com/blog/simplest-training) is 2–3 hard finger sessions weekly. The key insight for you: **your climbing days already count as finger-loading days**, so dedicated hangboard/block work should be layered onto climbing days (fingers-first, when fresh) rather than creating new high-load days.

**5. Progression models are largely practitioner-derived, with López's RPE/margin autoregulation the most evidence-connected.** López's "effort level/margin" (leaving 1–5s in reserve, typically 3s) is the best-validated autoregulation scheme; linear micro-loading (your ~1–1.5kg/week) works until plateau; block periodization (4-week blocks + deload) is standard coaching practice. Deload logic for tendon specifically is under-studied but consistent with the connective-tissue literature.

**6. Hypermobility genuinely changes the calculus — but loading remains beneficial.** Classic EDS shows patellar tendon stiffness ~50% lower than controls (Nielsen et al. 2014), though generalized/benign hypermobility without a collagen defect shows weaker or no stiffness deficit. Critically, lax/EDS tendons **do adapt** to progressive resistance training (patellar tendon stiffness rose 38.4% in a cEDS pilot) — the caveats are slower ramp-up, tighter joint-position control, and greater reliance on active muscular stabilization.

## Details

### Hangboarding protocols

**Max Hangs (MAW and MED).** Developed by Eva López. Low volume, high intensity: choose an edge (or added weight) allowing only 6–20s of hang; perform hangs of ~5–15s (typically ~10s) at an effort level leaving a ~3s margin, 2–5 sets, 3–5 min rest. Two variants: **MAW (Maximal Added Weight)** on a fixed edge (typically 18–20mm) and **MED (Minimum Edge Depth)** at bodyweight on progressively smaller edges. López's tested block: 4 weeks MAW (18mm) → 4 weeks MED, yielding ~15% strength gain at 4 weeks and ~28% at 8 weeks. *Mechanism:* primarily neural (motor-unit recruitment, coordination) in the short term, with longer-term tendon/pulley structural adaptation; the margin-based intensity control keeps the stimulus in the phosphagen/neural domain rather than the glycolytic/endurance domain. *Evidence:* strongest of any hangboard protocol; López's dead-hang test is validated against performance, showing "a significant positive correlation (r = 0.51, p < 0.001) between hang test results and climbers' redpoint grades." *Suits:* intermediate-to-advanced climbers with hangboard experience who can self-regulate load safely — not beginners. Recovery ~48h.

**Repeaters (López's "IntHangs").** Moderate intensity, higher volume, mimicking the work:rest of hard climbing sequences. Classic 7s-on/3s-off; López's IntHangs use lower volume with longer hangs/rests. *Mechanism:* strength-endurance via faster phosphocreatine resynthesis, muscle oxygenation, and some hypertrophy. *Evidence:* ~45% endurance gain over 8 weeks in López's cohort; excellent endurance tool, inferior to Max Hangs for peak strength. *Suits:* base-building, route/lead climbers, and as a gentler introduction to the board.

**Density hangs (Tyler Nelson) / SubHangs (López).** Longer holds (20–40s) at moderate intensity [StrengthClimbing](https://strengthclimbing.com/dr-tyler-nelsons-density-hangs-finger-training-for-rock-climbing/) (~40–70% or to failure). *Mechanism:* per Nelson, hypertrophy of muscle and muscle-tendon junction, increased MTJ stiffness while *decreasing* tendon stiffness — a net "less stiff overall but less injury-prone" system; [StrengthClimbing](https://strengthclimbing.com/dr-tyler-nelsons-density-hangs-finger-training-for-rock-climbing/) also proposed as a rehab/warm-up and connective-tissue conditioning tool. *Evidence:* mechanistic/theoretical plus practitioner experience; direct outcome trials are thin. *Suits:* base phases, tendon conditioning, injury-history climbers, and — relevant to you — connective-tissue-longevity goals.

**The Abrahangs / no-hang low-intensity daily model.** Emil & Felix Abrahamsson's protocol built on Keith Baar's engineered-ligament work: ~10-minute sessions at low intensity (feet on the ground, loading to "a light strain on their forearms, ~40% of max"), twice daily, ≥6h apart (exploiting the tendon's ~10-minute refractoriness and its need for a rest window to re-sensitize). *Evidence:* the 2024 Gilmore/Baar study (as strong as Max Hangs, additive when combined) — but note the in-vitro origin of the mechanism and the retrospective, unverified-load design. *Suits:* everyone as a low-risk adjunct; especially valuable for tissue health and for those wanting to add stimulus without adding peak load.

### Lifting pins / pinch blocks / block pulls

Also called no-hangs, arm-lifting, or farmer crimps. You grip a block/edge and lift a loading pin or weights off the ground, arm at the side. *How loading differs:* (a) load = plate weight only, decoupled from bodyweight, enabling both sub-bodyweight rehab loads and supramaximal loads on tiny grips; (b) no shoulder/body-tension/overhead demand; (c) grip reset and chalk-up between reps; (d) wrist angle is free, which is why blocks are decisively better for **pinch** (they force thumb adductor/flexor/opponens engagement rather than allowing lateral hangboard cheating) and for **finger-grouping/back-3** isolation; (e) more instability, slightly reducing peak force. Hooper's Beta and Lattice both note blocks are the tool of choice for rehab (precise, quantifiable, light loading) and for grips requiring loads far from bodyweight. [Hooper's Beta](https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/how-to-train-finger-strength-with-no-hangs)

*Typical progression (Hooper's Beta):* establish a 3RM by feel; add weight every couple of weeks; when you stall, drop ~2–5% and build reps into the 10–12 range or holds to 10–15s+, then resume adding load — i.e., alternating wave between load and volume. Run at least 4–6 weeks to reap benefits. [Hooper's Beta](https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/how-to-train-finger-strength-with-no-hangs) Lattice's general progression: increase intensity (weight) while holding sets/reps/TUT, or increase volume at fixed intensity; [Lattice Training](https://latticetraining.com/blog/how-to-manage-finger-strength-for-climbers) keep a protocol until plateau, then change. *Evidence relative to hangboards:* Schöffl's RCT (advanced climbers) found weighted hangboard training improved overall grip and multiple pinch measures more than climbing alone; [nih](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8241953/) there is no head-to-head RCT proving blocks superior or inferior to hangboards for edge strength — the honest position is they are comparably effective for the trained tissue, with blocks winning on load precision, joint control, pinch specificity, and rehab, and hangboards winning on climbing-chain specificity and edge "feel."

### Direct comparison

| Dimension | Hangboard (Max Hangs/MED) | Lifting pin/block pull |
|---|---|---|
| Load source | Bodyweight ± belt/pulley | Plate/pin only (fully decoupled) |
| Load precision at low end | Poor (needs pulley to deload) | Excellent (add small plates) |
| Peak/supramaximal loading | Good but less controllable | Excellent and controllable |
| Joint-position control | Lower (swing/shear) | Higher (stable, resettable) |
| Pinch specificity | Poor (thumb can be cheated) | Excellent |
| Crimp/edge "feel" & chain specificity | Highest | Lower (isolates flexors) |
| Rehab suitability | Lower | Highest |
| Shoulder/elbow load | Higher | Minimal |
| Injury risk profile | Higher peak/uncontrolled | Lower, titratable |

Grip-type specificity: half-crimp and open-hand should carry most training volume (open-hand is the most tendon-friendly, half-crimp the standard test position); [The Hangboard](https://thehangboard.com/blogs/news/finger-strength-climbing) full crimp produces the highest peak force but the highest pulley load and should be trained at lower volume on confident holds. Both modalities train isometrics; add overcoming-isometric recruitment pulls and velocity pulls (Nelson) for rate-of-force-development/contact strength that neither static hang nor slow block pull fully develops.

### Frequency and total tissue-load management

Anchor rules: ≥48h between hard finger sessions (48–72h after maximal work or lingering soreness); [Boulder Trainer](https://boulder-trainer.com/blog/how-often-hangboard/) ≤4 aggregate hard finger/climbing days weekly (Hörst); [Training For Climbing](https://trainingforclimbing.com/4-fingerboard-strength-protocols-that-work/) 2/week suffices for most, 3/week for advanced climbers who can *prove* recovery (no lingering soreness, no performance drop). [Boulder Trainer](https://boulder-trainer.com/blog/how-often-hangboard/) Both successful hangboard RCTs (Beastmaker-app study; Mundry et al.) used only 2 sessions/week. [Boulder Trainer](https://boulder-trainer.com/blog/how-often-hangboard/) For your Mon/Thu/Sat climbing + 3 lifting days: treat limit bouldering as a max-finger day; place high-intensity hangboard/block work at the *start* of a climbing day (fingers fresh) rather than opening a fourth high-load day; reserve low-intensity Abrahangs/density work for the gaps, since these are recoverable daily. The refractory-period logic (Baar: ~10 min of loading maximizes the collagen-synthesis signal; [BoxLife Magazine](https://boxlifemagazine.com/secret-static-holds-build-stronger-tendons/) ≥6–8h rest to re-sensitize; [Theelitetrainer](https://theelitetrainer.com/tendon-training-3-0-the-science-of-load-healing-and-hormonal-balance/) unloading costs ~15–20% of tendon collagen in 3 days, taking ~8 weeks to rebuild) [BoxLife Magazine](https://boxlifemagazine.com/secret-static-holds-build-stronger-tendons/) supports frequent *brief* low-load exposure over rare marathon sessions for tissue health.

### Progression models

- **Linear micro-loading** (your ~1–1.5kg/week on the board; blocks in 2–4-week waves): simplest, works until plateau; then switch stimulus.
- **RPE/margin autoregulation (López):** train to a defined effort level (e.g., 3s margin before failure). Best-validated; ideal for autoregulating around fatigue from concurrent lifting/climbing and for hypermobile tissue where you want to avoid true failure.
- **Block periodization:** 4-week blocks (e.g., MAW→MED, or density→max→repeaters) with a deload; matches López's tested structure and Lattice's standard cycle.
- **Undulating:** alternate max and repeater/density stimuli within a week — useful for a hybrid athlete juggling multiple qualities.
- **Deload:** every 4th week or at plateau/soreness; connective-tissue-specific deload evidence is thin but consistent with tendon remodeling timelines.

### Applicability and individualization — including hypermobility

**Training age & baseline:** advanced climbers past the coordination-adaptation phase need supramaximal loads to keep driving recruitment [Climbing](https://www.climbing.com/skills/fingerboard-training-is-for-novice-climbers-part-1/) — this is where Max Hangs and heavy block pulls earn their place. Beginners should not start on Max Hangs.

**Injury history:** blocks/no-hangs and pulley-assisted hangs are the rehab tools (light, precise, quantifiable). [Tension Climbing](https://tensionclimbing.com/blogs/blog/death-taxes-and-the-desire-for-stronger-fingers-a-three-part-series-on-no-hang-training) Density/low-load high-frequency work conditions tissue with low peak stress.

**Hypermobility (your case):**
- *Tissue properties:* stiffness deficits are well-documented in classic EDS (~50% lower patellar tendon stiffness/Young's modulus; Nielsen et al. 2014, *FASEB Journal*) but **not clearly established in benign/generalized hypermobility without a collagen defect** — so don't assume your tendons are "weaker," but do respect that lax joints rely more on active muscular control.
- *Loading is beneficial:* lax/EDS tendons adapt to progressive resistance training — a cEDS pilot (Møller/Nielsen 2014, *Muscles, Ligaments and Tendons Journal*) saw patellar tendon stiffness rise 38.4% and maximal deformation drop 14.9% with 3×/week training, using a 12-session familiarization before load progression and no serious adverse events. Translation: **load, but ramp slowly and extend your familiarization period.**
- *Grip-position bias:* hypermobile/EDS climbers characteristically **over-crimp** (full crimp for everything) because it feels stable, [The Climbing Project](https://www.theclimbingproject.com/blog/climbing-with-ehlers-danlos-syndrome-physical-therapy-approaches-xhdj4) but full crimp couples ~90° PIP flexion with DIP hyperextension — exactly the pulley-strain-maximizing, end-range-loading position lax fingers should minimize. Bias toward open-hand (≈39° PIP) and half-crimp (≈70° PIP); reserve full crimp for low-volume, confident holds.
- *Modality choice:* block pulls are strongly indicated — fine sub-bodyweight load control, quantifiable slow progression, [La Fabrique Verticale](https://lafabriqueverticale.com/en/finger-strength-no-hanging-vs-hangboarding/) controlled joint position, reduced swing/shear, and better proprioceptive "feel" of finger engagement suit lax tissue well (Hooper's Beta; La Fabrique Verticale).
- *Active stabilization:* prioritize intrinsic-hand and finger co-contraction work in neutral range, proprioceptive/scapular/core stability, and consider taping/bracing lax finger joints as "secondary bracing" (per The Climbing Doctor: muscle contractions provide "a more dynamic form of stability… a secondary form of bracing to maintain joint positions in optimal alignment"). Muscular stiffness is your compensatory joint-stability mechanism.
- *Recovery:* hypermobile athletes may recover more slowly and lose strength-endurance faster with detraining — err toward the conservative end of frequency.

**Collagen nutrition:** Baar/Shaw protocol — ~15g hydrolyzed collagen or gelatin + ~50mg (up to ~225mg) vitamin C, 30–60 min before loading. Shaw et al. (2017, *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition*), a randomized double-blind crossover in 8 healthy men, found 15g vitamin-C-enriched gelatin taken 1h before intermittent loading "roughly doubled blood markers of collagen synthesis (PINP)" — raising circulating PINP by 153% at 4h vs ~54–59% for placebo/5g. Caveats: evidence is in healthy/tendinopathy subjects, measures biomarkers not injury outcomes, is loading-dependent, and has **not** been directly validated in hypermobile/EDS populations. Low downside; reasonable to trial given your longevity goals.

### Evidence vs. consensus — honest ledger
- **Evidence-based:** Max Hangs > repeaters for peak strength; repeaters > Max Hangs for endurance (López RCTs); hangboarding > climbing alone for finger strength (multiple RCTs); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1080603223001102) low-intensity finger loading can match max-load gains and combining is additive (Gilmore/Baar 2024, retrospective); [upi](https://oalib-perpustakaan.upi.edu/Record/doaj_c74da81d84d54ec0b07a29368544b687/Description?print=1) crimp loads the A2 pulley 3–4× fingertip force / 380–700 N (Miro/Schöffl 2021 biomechanics); lax tendons adapt to progressive loading (EDS studies); collagen+vitamin C pre-loading raises synthesis markers (Shaw 2017).
- **Practitioner opinion/mechanistic:** density-hang tendon-remodeling claims; recruitment/velocity-pull prescriptions; specific block-pull progression schemes; deload timing for tendon; the ~10-minute refractory window applied to human finger tendons (extrapolated from in-vitro engineered ligaments); most hypermobility-specific finger prescriptions (clinical reasoning, not trials).
- **Anecdotal:** Emil's own 2600%/30-day results (n=1); many online block-pull vs hangboard testimonials.

## Recommendations

**Stage 1 — Keep your dual-modality structure; it is supported.** Your two-day split (Max Hangs/Repeaters with López RPE framing + Lattice-style pinch/block with back-3 work) is precisely the additive combination the 2024 evidence supports. No case exists for specializing into one modality; the evidence and Lattice's own practice favor complementary use.

**Stage 2 — Audit total weekly finger load, not modality count.** Map every finger-loading exposure across your Mon/Thu/Sat climbing + 3 lift days. Ensure ≤3 genuinely hard finger days/week with ≥48h between them (limit bouldering counts). Put your heaviest hangboard/block work at the *start* of climbing days when fresh. Slot low-intensity Abrahangs/density work into the gaps — these are recoverable daily and serve your tissue-health goal.

**Stage 3 — Bias the plan for hypermobility.** (a) Keep most volume in open-hand/half-crimp; cap full-crimp volume and only on confident holds. (b) Favor block pulls for their joint-position control and load precision, especially for pinch and back-3. (c) Progress slower than a non-hypermobile peer — hold your ~1–1.5kg/week only while form and joint control stay clean; extend familiarization at each new edge/grip. (d) Use López's margin (never train edge grips to true failure). (e) Add dedicated intrinsic-hand and finger-stabilizer work in neutral range plus proprioceptive/scapular work. (f) Trial the Baar collagen+vitamin C pre-load.

**Stage 4 — Program tendon-health blocks explicitly.** Given longevity is a first-class goal, periodize a density-hang/SubHang or Abrahangs emphasis block (2–4 weeks) between max-strength blocks, and keep a low-load daily exposure running year-round for connective-tissue conditioning.

**Benchmarks that change the plan:**
- *Escalate/deload trigger:* any lingering finger-joint soreness >48h, a drop in climbing performance, or DIP/PIP joint pain (as opposed to muscle/forearm fatigue) → cut a hard finger day, drop to open-hand only, or insert a deload.
- *Progress trigger:* if load stalls for ~3–4 weeks with clean recovery, switch stimulus (wave load↔volume on blocks; MAW↔MED on the board) rather than forcing more weight.
- *Frequency trigger:* only add a 3rd hard finger day if you have zero lingering soreness and stable climbing performance for several weeks.

## Caveats
- The climbing finger-training literature is small; most protocol comparisons rest on a handful of studies (largely López's group) with modest sample sizes, and the 2024 Gilmore/Baar study is retrospective with self-selected, unverified loads and modest effect sizes.
- The Baar "10-minute refractory / 6–8h rest" tendon model derives from in-vitro engineered ligaments; direct human finger-tendon confirmation is lacking, and Hooper's Beta and others rightly flag the translational gap.
- No head-to-head RCT directly compares lifting pins/blocks vs hangboards for edge strength; the "comparably effective" conclusion is inference from mechanism, the Schöffl weighted-hang RCT, and coaching consensus.
- Hypermobility findings are strongest for genetically-verified classic EDS; extrapolation to generalized/benign hypermobility (likely your situation unless formally diagnosed) is uncertain, and most finger-specific hypermobility prescriptions are clinical reasoning rather than trial-tested. If you have a formal hEDS/HSD diagnosis or frequent subluxations, individualized assessment is warranted.
- Density-hang tendon-remodeling mechanisms and collagen-supplement injury-prevention benefits are plausible but not proven by outcome trials.
