# Bulletproofing Wrists and Shoulders for Climbing: An Evidence-Weighted Protocol for a Hybrid Athlete (with Hypermobility Considerations)

## TL;DR
- **The highest-return, most time-efficient investment is load, not variety:** 2–3 short (10–20 min) sessions per week of progressive resistance for (a) shoulder external rotators/posterior cuff + scapular upward rotators (serratus, lower trap) and (b) the six wrist actions (flex/extend, pronate/supinate, radial/ulnar deviate), bolted onto the ends of days you already train. Tack tendon-specific isometric "creep" work onto the exact structures that carry your history/risk (biceps/medial elbow/wrist). Everything else is optional volume.
- **Progress connective tissue differently from muscle:** tendons/ligaments/capsule adapt to *magnitude and duration of load* on a slower timeline (weeks-to-months, not sessions). Use heavy-slow-resistance (6-second reps) and sustained isometrics, hold loads for 2–3 weeks before advancing, and judge readiness by the 24-hour pain/stiffness response, not by how the muscle feels.
- **Hypermobility flips the emphasis but not the direction:** the best RCT evidence (Liaghat/Shoulder-MOBILEX) shows heavy, full-range strengthening is *safe and short-term superior* to gentle low-load work for hypermobile shoulders — so hypermobile climbers should still load hard, but prioritise closed-chain/isometric control, mid-range over end-range, tempo control over ballistic reps, taping/proprioceptive feedback, and avoid stretching already-lax joints.

## Key Findings

**1. Where the injuries actually are (so you train the right things).** Upper-extremity injuries dominate climbing — Leung's AMSSM "Rock Climbing Injuries Basics" gives the distribution as upper extremity ~70% (finger highest prevalence), lower extremity ~20%, other ~10%; Jones/Schöffl-derived figures in the hand-surgery literature put it as roughly 75% of climbers having acute or chronic upper-extremity injury, ~60% involving the hand/wrist and ~40% the shoulder/elbow. A 2025 recreational-climber survey (Boute et al., *J Hand Surg Glob Online*; n=745) found 77% reported at least one injury, most commonly to the fingers/hands (70%), with higher odds in males, those with more experience, and — notably — those using a fingerboard. Shoulder pathology in climbers clusters around the *anterior/superior* structures: subacromial impingement, rotator-cuff tendinopathy, SLAP (superior labral) tears, and long-head-of-biceps tendinopathy — all driven by climbing's internal-rotation, adduction-dominant, overhead-loaded movement pattern. Schöffl et al.'s MRI study of 31 climbers with ~30 years at a high level (*J Shoulder Elbow Surg* 2021) found labral tears, biceps-tendon abnormalities, cartilage lesions and osteoarthritic changes significantly more frequent than in matched controls, correlating with climbing intensity but *not* with clinical exam findings — every climber had at least 8 of 20 possible abnormalities, yet only 16% of shoulders were symptomatic (lifetime shoulder-pain prevalence 77%). At the wrist, the dominant overuse problems are TFCC (ulnar-sided) injury and extensor carpi ulnaris issues, plus dorsal impingement. This epidemiology tells you where to spend limited training time: **posterior cuff/external rotation and scapular control for the shoulder; rotational and ulnar-sided stability for the wrist.**

**2. The single most important efficiency principle: match the exercise to the climbing demand, and load it properly.** Dr. Jared Vagy (The Climbing Doctor, DPT, USC, board-certified orthopaedic clinical specialist) argues most climbers train the cuff wrong — standing internal/external rotations at the torso don't reflect climbing demand; the exercise must mirror overhead, loaded positions. [TrainingBeta](https://www.trainingbeta.com/strengthen-rotator-cuff-climbing/) His climbing-specific staples are external rotation progressed into the 90°/90° position, prone Y/T/I work, and scaption. Physiologically this is backed by Reinold et al.'s EMG work showing side-lying external rotation maximally recruits infraspinatus/teres minor, [E3 Rehab](https://e3rehab.com/rotatorcuff/) and by evidence that overhead rehab should be done in the *scapular plane* (20–30° forward of the frontal plane) to maximise scapular upward rotation and reduce cuff impingement. [nih](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC155505) For scapular stabilisers, prone external rotation at 90° abduction, prone flexion and high scapular retraction give the best lower-trapezius ratios, and serratus is best hit with protraction/"push-up plus," diagonal punches [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4886800/) and overhead carries.

**3. Wrists: train all six actions, cheaply and briefly.** The Hooper's Beta ("bulletproof wrists") and Lattice protocols converge on the same content: wrist flexion, extension, pronation, supination, plus radial/ulnar deviation (the latter often trained via hammer curls and triceps extensions), thumb/pinch for wrist stability, and reverse curls for flexor-extensor co-contraction. [hoopersbeta](https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/how-to-get-bulletproof-wrists-in-15-minutes) Hooper's programming default is **2 sets × 8–12 reps at 2–3 reps-in-reserve, 1–2×/week building to 2–3×/week, done *after* climbing.** Lattice's device work (Heavy Roller, wrist-rotation pin) targets sloper/wrist stability and doubles as tennis/golfer's-elbow rehab. Both emphasise that wrist strength reduces the friction demand on slopers and offloads the fingers/pulleys down the chain.

**4. Connective tissue adapts on a different clock — this is the crux of "progression."** Muscle responds to progressive overload session-to-session; tendon/ligament/capsule respond to the *magnitude and duration* of strain and remodel far more slowly. Three research strands matter:
- **Isometrics (Rio, Cook, Purdam):** Rio et al. (*Br J Sports Med* 2015; n=6 volleyball players with patellar tendinopathy) found a heavy isometric bout (5 × 45 s at ~70% MVC) dropped pain from ~7/10 to 0, sustained for at least 45 minutes, alongside reduced cortical inhibition and a 19% rise in maximal voluntary isometric contraction. Useful for pain modulation and for loading a cranky tendon (biceps, medial elbow, wrist) when you still need to climb — though later replication (e.g., O'Neill's Achilles work, *KSSTA* 2019) shows clear "responder/non-responder" variability, so treat it as a tool, not a guarantee.
- **Heavy Slow Resistance (Kongsgaard/Malliaras/Cook):** ~70% 1RM [clinicaltrials](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/18/NCT04578418/SAP_000.pdf) (roughly 6RM–15RM depending on phase), 6-second reps (3 s up/3 s down), 3–4 sets, ~3×/week, is as effective as eccentric protocols with better adherence, [clinicaltrials](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/18/NCT04578418/SAP_000.pdf) and improves tendon stiffness and load tolerance. Malliaras/Cook network reviews conclude *no protocol is clearly superior* — the common denominator is progressive, monitored load.
- **The Baar "10-minute" connective-tissue rule:** Keith Baar's engineered-ligament work (*Sports Med* 2017, "Minimizing Injury and Maximizing Return to Play") states that "sinews, like bone, quickly become refractory to an exercise stimulus, suggesting that short (<10 min) periods of activity with relatively long (6 h) periods of rest are best to train these tissues" — splitting loading into short bouts with ≥6–8 h rest roughly doubled collagen synthesis in vitro. A practical micro-dose (BraceLab, summarising Baar) is "1 set of 6–12 reps, for 10–30 seconds… at a 2/10 rate of perceived exertion, spaced at least 6 hours apart." **Caveat: this rests largely on in-vitro/engineered-tissue and small case-series data — Baar's own collaborators note there are no large clinical trials yet.** [Humanperformancealliance](https://humanperformancealliance.org/playbook/how-to-improve-our-tendon-health-with-training/) It's a reasonable, low-cost heuristic (short, frequent, submaximal isometric "creep" sessions; consider ~15 g hydrolysed collagen [Doctor Yaad](https://doctoryaad.com/articles/how-to-get-bulletproof-tendons-ft-professor-keith-baar-doctor-yaad-podcast) + vitamin C ~30–60 min before) but should be presented as emerging, not established.
- **Timelines:** tendon collagen synthesis rises and turns over with loading but structural change lags; immobilisation is disproportionately costly [Doctor Yaad](https://doctoryaad.com/articles/how-to-get-bulletproof-tendons-ft-professor-keith-baar-doctor-yaad-podcast) — Baar-derived summaries cite ~20% collagen loss within three days of unloading, plateauing at that reduced state within two weeks and taking far longer to rebuild. Practically: expect **weeks-to-months** for durability gains, hold each load level ~2–3 weeks, and treat the 24-h response as your progression gate.

**5. Hypermobility changes the picture — but "load it" still wins.** The best evidence is the Danish **Shoulder-MOBILEX RCT** (Liaghat et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2022;56(22):1269–76; NCT03869307; n=100 HSD/hEDS with ≥3-month shoulder complaints, 79% women, mean age 37.8, mean Beighton 5.8). High-load, full-range strengthening (5 exercises: side-lying ER neutral, prone horizontal abduction, prone ER at 90° abduction, supine scapular protraction, seated scaption; progressed 50%→70%→90% of 10RM over 3 weeks, then 3×10RM weeks 4–9, then 4×8RM weeks 10–15, dumbbells up to 15 kg, tapering to 70% in week 16) was **statistically superior** to gentle low-load band work at 16 weeks (adjusted WOSI between-group difference −174.5 points, 95% CI −341.4 to −7.7; NNT = 3). The pre-specified minimally clinically important difference was 252 WOSI points: the intention-to-treat estimate (−174.5) fell short of it, but per-protocol adherers reached ~−250.7 points — essentially the MCID. There were **no serious adverse events** — heavy loading caused more transient soreness (56% vs 37%) and headaches (40% vs 20%), and instability events were minor and actually slightly *fewer* in the heavy group (1 subluxation + 1 dislocation vs 3 subluxations in the low-load group). At 1 year (Liaghat et al., *Br J Sports Med* 2024;58(7):373–381) both groups had improved (~62% overall reaching a clinically important WOSI change) and largely held their gains, and the heavy group was **no longer statistically superior** (WOSI difference −92.9, 95% CI −257.4 to 71.5, p=0.268), though heavy retained a significant edge on the emotional/confidence subdomain. **Bottom line: hypermobile shoulders tolerate and benefit from heavy, full-range strengthening; the main practical difference is emphasis and monitoring, not avoidance of load.**

Hypermobility-specific nuances the climbing and EDS literature agree on:
- **Risk profile shifts** from sprains toward *subluxation/dislocation, capsular laxity, proprioceptive deficits, tendinopathy and slower recovery.* The Climbing Doctor's review (citing Nathan et al., *BMJ Open Sport Exerc Med* 2018) notes joint dislocation was experienced *only* by hypermobile athletes, while ligament/joint sprains were actually *more* common in non-hypermobile athletes — hypermobile joints already sit near end-range, so they dislocate rather than sprain. Generalised joint hypermobility is associated with [The Climbing Doctor](https://theclimbingdoctor.com/hypermobility-strength-or-weakness/) measurably poorer joint position sense (elbow/knee shown directly) [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12627509/) and reduced muscle endurance at higher Beighton scores [PubMed Central](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12627509/) , while grip strength is often preserved or even higher — i.e., a proprioceptive/endurance deficit, not a strength deficit.
- **The isometric-position evidence** (Spanhove/De Wandele, Ghent; *Physiotherapy* 2020): in hEDS/HSD shoulders with multidirectional instability, isometric external rotation and light dumbbell loading produced a *stabilising inferior* humeral-head translation, whereas isometric flexion and shoulder/elbow extension produced anterior-superior/superior (destabilising) translation. This supports **prioritising external-rotation and controlled loaded holds** and being cautious with positions that drive the head anteriorly.
- **Closed-chain and isometric before end-range/open-chain:** hypermobility physiotherapy consensus (Ehlers-Danlos Support UK, HMSA, paediatric CSP guidance, EDS clinics) is to build *active* dynamic stability, improve scapulohumeral coordination, use closed-chain (wall/table weight-bearing) and rhythmic stabilisation, pace carefully to avoid flares (start ~5–10 min, 2–3×/week), and — importantly — **not to stretch already-lax joints** (preserve some muscle stiffness; avoid end-range/ballistic work). Central sensitisation is more common in hEDS/HSD, so a disproportionate pain response to normal loading is a recognised phenomenon that shouldn't automatically halt progression.

## Details

### Programme architecture (fits a 3× climb / 3× PPL lifting week)
Treat this as **two ~15-minute "durability blocks"** plus optional daily tendon micro-dosing — not extra full sessions.

**Block A — Shoulder (2×/week).** Best attached to your Pull and Push days (cuff/scap work pairs naturally and won't rob finger/pull performance if done at the end).
- **External rotation / posterior cuff:** side-lying ER (neutral) → progress to 90/90 prone ER or cable ER at 90° abduction. 3–4 sets × 10–15; this is your non-negotiable, climbing-specific anti-impingement lift and, per the Ghent data, the ER position that actively *centres* a lax humeral head.
- **Scapular upward rotators / serratus:** prone Y and T (thumbs up), scaption in the scapular plane (thumbs up, 20–30° forward), plus a serratus drill (push-up-plus or overhead/waiter carry). 2–3 sets × 10–15.
- **Posterior-chain balance:** face-pulls or prone horizontal abduction to counter climbing's internal-rotation/adduction bias.
- Because you already run a structured PPL, fold much of this into existing pull volume; the additive cost is really just the cuff and serratus isolation.

**Block B — Wrist/forearm (2×/week, after climbing).** Hooper's superset template: (1) supination + flexion, (2) extension + pronation, (3) hammer curl + triceps extension (radial/ulnar deviation), (4) pinch/reverse curl/kettlebell-bottoms-up. [hoopersbeta](https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/how-to-get-bulletproof-wrists-in-15-minutes) 2 sets × 8–12 (5–8 for pinch), 2–3 RIR. [hoopersbeta](https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/how-to-get-bulletproof-wrists-in-15-minutes) A single lever/rotation tool [Hooper's Beta](https://www.hoopersbeta.com/store/p/torque-pro) (Lattice pin, Hooper's Torque, or a hammer/PVC + plates) covers all six actions cheaply. This complements — does not duplicate — your existing Eva López max-hangs and Lattice pinch protocol, which train finger flexors and thumb but *not* wrist extensors, rotators or deviators. The bottoms-up kettlebell press is a two-for-one: wrist-stabiliser co-contraction plus scapular-plane overhead shoulder stimulus (skip it if you have active shoulder symptoms).

**Tendon micro-dosing (optional, most valuable if you have a specific niggle).** For a specific at-risk/irritable tendon (long-head biceps, medial epicondyle, ulnar wrist): 1 set of ~6–12 reps of 10–30 s isometric holds at ~2/10 perceived exertion for health/maintenance (or heavier 5×45 s at ~70% for acute pain relief), ideally split into short bouts ≥6 h apart [BraceLab](https://bracelab.com/clinicians-classroom/reconsidering-isometrics-insights-from-the-work-of-dr-keith-baar) per Baar's model. This is the "load as medicine" layer that keeps you climbing through minor tendinopathy.

### Sequencing, tempo, deload
- **Where in the session:** cuff/scap and wrist work go *at the end* (after climbing and after heavy compound lifts) so they don't blunt performance; Hooper explicitly recommends after climbing, never before. [hoopersbeta](https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/how-to-get-bulletproof-wrists-in-15-minutes)
- **Tempo:** for connective-tissue emphasis use slow 6-second reps (HSR) or sustained isometrics; for hypermobility, controlled tempo (~1 s up / 2 s down, as used in the shoulder trials) and *no ballistic reps*.
- **Deload:** mirror your hangboard periodisation — hold a load 2–3 weeks, then step up; every ~4–6 weeks take a lighter week (the MOBILEX trial itself tapered to 70% in its final week before testing). [Physiotutors](https://www.physiotutors.com/research/loading-of-hypermobile-shoulders/) Progress load *before* reps once past the 8–15 rep familiarisation.

### Progression rules specific to tendon/ligament/capsule
1. **Advance by load magnitude and time-under-tension, slowly.** Move HSR from ~15RM → ~6RM over ~9–12 weeks (the classic Kongsgaard structure the MOBILEX trial mirrored).
2. **Use the 24-hour rule as the gate:** pain/stiffness that settles to baseline by next morning and is stable-or-decreasing = progress; pain that climbs with added load = regress one level (Cook/Physiopedia load-management framework: increasing load with pain trending to 0 is ideal, low-stable pain is acceptable, rising pain is unacceptable).
3. **Isometrics as the regression/holding pattern** when irritable; HSR/eccentric as the build; heavier/faster/closed-to-open-chain as the return-to-climbing bridge.
4. **Don't immobilise** — even irritated tendons want gentle load early, [Doctor Yaad](https://doctoryaad.com/articles/how-to-get-bulletproof-tendons-ft-professor-keith-baar-doctor-yaad-podcast) given the rapid collagen loss (~20% within three days) with disuse.

### Comparative synthesis — where sources agree, disagree, and where evidence is thin
- **Strong agreement:** load beats rest/passive modalities for tendon; external rotation/posterior cuff + scapular control is the right shoulder target for climbers; wrists should be trained through all six actions; hypermobile joints benefit from strengthening and proprioceptive/closed-chain work rather than stretching.
- **Genuine disagreement / equivalence:** isometric vs eccentric vs HSR — network meta-analyses find no clear winner; isometric analgesia is real but inconsistent across individuals and tendons. [clinicaltrials](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT03848598) High-load vs low-load in hypermobility is *superior short-term but equivalent by 1 year* — both "work," so adherence and preference matter as much as intensity.
- **Emerging / thin evidence (flagged honestly):** Baar's 10-minute/short-bout collagen protocol is mechanistically compelling but rests on in-vitro and case-series data, not RCTs; collagen+vitamin-C timing is plausible but small-effect. Climbing-*specific* hypermobility injury data barely exists — most inference is borrowed from dance, football, netball and general EDS cohorts, which themselves conflict (some show more injuries/severity in hypermobile athletes, others no difference). [theclimbingdoctor](https://theclimbingdoctor.com/hypermobility-strength-or-weakness/) The climbing shoulder-MRI degeneration study is small (n=31) and cross-sectional. Treat wrist/shoulder climbing-prehab specifics as expert-informed practice, not high-level RCT evidence.

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — Screen (week 1).** Self-administer the Beighton score (≥5/9 in adults suggests generalised hypermobility, but a low score doesn't exclude it — shoulder/wrist laxity can be present with a modest score) and note any history of subluxation, clicking, or slow recovery. If you have frank instability/apprehension, get one assessment from a climbing-literate physio before loading heavy overhead — the single highest-value hour you can spend.

**Stage 1 — Establish (weeks 1–4).** Add Block A (shoulder) to two lifting days and Block B (wrist) after two climbing days. Start at the "familiarisation" end: 2–3 sets × 10–15 reps, controlled tempo, 2–3 RIR. Goal is groove and tolerance, not load. If hypermobile: begin with isometric ER (fist-into-wall) [Hooper's Beta](https://www.hoopersbeta.com/library/external-rotator-levels-1-25) and closed-chain (table/wall weight-bearing) plus side-lying ER before open-chain overhead.

**Stage 2 — Build (weeks 5–12).** Shift cuff/scap and wrist work toward strength: fewer reps, more load, 6-second tempo on at least one exercise per block; progress load only when the 24-h response is clean. Introduce the 90/90 external-rotation position and scaption-to-overhead in the scapular plane. Layer in tendon isometric micro-dosing for any specific niggle.

**Stage 3 — Durability/maintenance (ongoing).** Two brief blocks/week maintains gains; you can drop to 1–2×/week for wrists once strong. Re-test loads every ~6 weeks; deload one week in ~6.

**Thresholds that change the plan:**
- *Pain rising with added load, or morning stiffness not settling* → regress to isometrics, cut provocative load, reassess in 1–2 weeks.
- *Any true subluxation/dislocation, night pain, catching/locking (possible SLAP), or ulnar-wrist pain that limits weight-bearing (possible TFCC)* → stop loading that pattern, brace/tape, and see a hand/shoulder-competent physio; these are not "train-through" problems.
- *Hypermobile + disproportionate pain flares* → suspect central sensitisation; pace with smaller baselines and longer ramps rather than abandoning load.
- *Plateau in climbing-specific stability despite gym strength* → add closed-chain/proprioceptive and rhythmic-stabilisation work and unstable-load carries (bottoms-up kettlebell), which target the position-sense deficit that raw strength misses.

## Caveats
- **Not medical advice.** Given the subluxation/labral/capsule risks in a hypermobility picture, a one-off in-person assessment by a climbing-familiar physiotherapist (e.g., someone using Vagy's Rock Rehab framework or equivalent) is genuinely worth it before progressing to heavy overhead loading, and to individualise which end-ranges are safe for *your* joints.
- **Evidence quality is uneven.** Shoulder and wrist tendinopathy loading science is strong but largely extrapolated from lower-limb (patellar/Achilles) tendons. The hypermobile-shoulder RCT evidence is genuinely good but short-to-medium term and in a general (non-climbing) population. Climbing-specific and hypermobility-in-climbing data are sparse; much here is best-practice inference, and I've flagged where that's the case.
- **The Baar connective-tissue protocol is promising but pre-clinical** — use it as a low-risk heuristic, not a proven prescription.
- **Individual variation dominates.** Isometric analgesia, response to heavy load, and end-range tolerance all vary person-to-person, especially with hypermobility. Your 24-hour symptom response and climbing quality are better guides than any generic rep scheme.
