# Training Around Hypermobility for Climbers: Elbow Hyperextension, Mantling, and Upper-Body Stability

## TL;DR
- The evidence-based priority for hypermobile climbers is **building muscular control, proprioception, and strength around loose joints — not stretching them** — because muscles provide the dynamic "secondary brace" that lax ligaments cannot; supervised, progressive resistance training (including heavy loading) is well tolerated and improves pain, strength, and proprioception in people with generalized joint hypermobility.
- For your specific elbow problem, the core fix is learning **"active" muscular lockout** (co-contracting biceps and triceps to hold the elbow just short of end range) instead of "false stability" — passively hanging into the hyperextended stop, which switches the surrounding muscles off and leaves the joint most vulnerable. Use eccentric biceps work (the biceps as a "brake"), mid-range isometrics, and closed-chain proprioceptive drills.
- To rebuild pressing/mantle strength without lockout hyperextension, **regress the push-up and dip to controlled ranges** — incline/box push-ups stopped short of full extension, floor presses (range limited by the floor), box/bench dips, banded press-downs and controlled tricep extensions — and train the mantle as a skill in low-consequence positions while pairing it with scapular, rotator-cuff, and wrist stability work.

## Key Findings

**1. The governing principle: strengthen and stabilize, don't stretch.** Multiple physiotherapy sources converge on the same message for symptomatic hypermobility (generalized joint hypermobility, hypermobility spectrum disorder, and hEDS): the treatment is muscular strengthening, proprioceptive/neuromuscular retraining, and end-range control — not increasing the joint's range. The Climbing Doctor (Dr. Jared Vagy's site) puts it plainly: muscles provide "a secondary form of bracing to maintain joint positions in optimal alignment," and exercises should be "performed within a safe range that does not push the joint outside its normal limits."

**2. Proprioception is the measurable deficit — and it responds to training.** Hypermobile joints have demonstrably poorer position sense. Ferrell WR, Tennant N, Sturrock RD, et al. ("Amelioration of symptoms by enhancement of proprioception in patients with joint hypermobility syndrome," *Arthritis & Rheumatism* 2004;50(10):3323–3328) showed that an 8-week home program of progressive closed-kinetic-chain exercises produced results in which "proprioceptive acuity increased in 16 of 18 subjects and was very significantly improved overall (P<0.001)," with parallel gains on the balance board, quadriceps/hamstring strength, pain (P=0.003), and quality of life (P=0.029 for physical functioning; P=0.008 for mental health). More recently, Akaras E et al. ("The effects of joint hypermobility on strength, proprioception, and functional performance," *Scientific Reports* 2025), studying 83 adults, found that hypermobile individuals "demonstrated significantly poorer proprioception at the elbow and knee (p<0.05) but no differences in grip strength or functional performance" — meaning the core problem in hypermobile elbows is sensory awareness, not raw weakness.

**3. Resistance training works — but intensity and supervision matter.** Two studies illustrate the dose–response. In a 12-week *self-guided, low-intensity* single-blind RCT in women with generalized joint hypermobility (Beighton ≥6/9; *BMC Sports Science, Medicine and Rehabilitation* 2021), "no improvement in strength or muscle mass by self-guided resistance training was found," with the authors attributing this to "low resistance levels, as well as the choice of outcome measures"; effect sizes actually slightly favored the control group. By contrast, Henriksen P, Junge T, Bojsen-Møller J, Juul-Kristensen B, Thorlund JB ("Supervised, Heavy Resistance Training Is Tolerated and Potentially Beneficial in Women with Knee Pain and Knee Joint Hypermobility: A Case Series," *Translational Sports Medicine* 2022;2022:8367134), in which 16 women (mean age 24.2, SD 2.5) completed a supervised progressive heavy program (5 exercises, sets to failure, 2×/week for 12 weeks), reported that "no major adverse events were observed," pain during the aggravating activity fell by a mean of 32.5 mm on a 100-mm scale (95% CI 21.4–43.6, p<0.001), 5-repetition-max strength rose 39–82%, single-leg hop improved ~16%, knee proprioception error dropped ~55%, and patellar tendon stiffness rose ~11%. The lesson: hypermobile bodies tolerate and benefit from progressive loading, but it needs to be well-guided and progressed to meaningful intensity.

**4. "False stability" / passive lockout is the specific mechanism behind your elbow issue.** Hypermobility educator Jeannie Di Bon describes locking or snapping the elbow into hyperextension as "false stability": "When we lock out our elbows, the muscles around the joint no longer have to work, making the joint vulnerable to strain and pain." [jeanniedibon](https://jeanniedibon.com/what-to-do-with-hypermobile-elbow-joints/) The joint feels stable but is actually at its most vulnerable, and locking also "turns off your powerhouse of muscles by blocking communication to the torso." [jeanniedibon](https://jeanniedibon.com/what-to-do-with-hypermobile-elbow-joints/) This is exactly what happens to a hypermobile climber who "hangs" into a straight-arm mantle.

**5. Eccentric and mid-range work builds the "muscular brake."** The clinical approach for hyperextending elbows strengthens biceps/triceps *within mid-range* while using eccentric biceps loading so the biceps, in the words of the HandLab/BraceLab clinical pearl, "act as a brake during extension, allowing the patient to actively prevent elbow hyperextension" (example: slowly lowering a weight after a biceps curl). In the underlying case report — Kaux JF, Forthomme B, Foidart-Dessalle M, et al., "Eccentric Training for Elbow Hypermobility," *Journal of Novel Physiotherapies* 2013 — an Ehlers-Danlos tennis player was treated with 18 sessions (3×/week) of graded eccentric isokinetic work at slow speeds within a limited range; the authors reported that "isokinetic evaluation objectified a significant improvement in maximal torque in flexion-extension muscles of the right elbow" (their write-up cites an improvement of roughly 20–25%), with the exercises serving to "safely reinforce the muscles as a protective brake for joint hypermobility."

**6. Mantling is a pull-to-push skill with distinct phases and known failure points.** Mantling transitions from pulling to pressing, driving the hips up and over a high foot, then pressing down through the triceps to stand. Coaching sources identify the "chicken wing" (elbow flares, shoulder collapses into internal rotation) and the "beached whale" (hips stay low) as the classic failures — both of which get worse if you lack controlled pressing strength and default to a passive locked arm.

## Details

### General principles of exercise-based management (physiotherapy/sports-medicine + climbing sources)

- **Strengthen the periarticular muscles to create dynamic stability.** The mechanism, per physiotherapy sources, is "strengthening the periarticular muscles to ensure stability, improving proprioception... and developing neuromuscular control to prevent excessive movement." Because lax ligaments "cannot be tightened," muscle becomes the compensating stabilizer.
- **Prioritize proprioception and end-range control.** Closed-chain and proprioceptive programs have the best evidence base in hypermobility (Ferrell 2004). Train awareness of where "neutral" is so you can stop short of the hyperextended stop.
- **Favor closed kinetic chain (CKC) work for stability and proprioception.** CKC exercises (hand/foot fixed, e.g. planks, push-up positions, weight-bearing drills) enhance joint compression, proximal stability, proprioception, and motor-unit synchronization [mTrigger](https://mtrigger.com/blogs/latest-articles/open-vs-closed-kinetic-chain-exercise-for-the-upper-extremity) — highly relevant since climbing is largely closed-chain. Open-chain work (free-moving limb, e.g. curls, band press-downs) is still valuable for isolating specific muscles and controlling load precisely, especially early on. [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_kinetic_chain_exercises)
- **Use isometrics as the entry point.** Isometric holds load muscle and tendon without moving the joint into problematic range, are often the most tolerable starting point for flare-prone or deconditioned hypermobile people, [The Fibro Guy](https://www.thefibroguy.com/exercise-rehabilitation-hypermobility-guide/) and have an analgesic (pain-reducing) effect in tendinopathy. Isometric loading also drives collagen synthesis and increases tendon stiffness over ~12 weeks. [Phyba](https://phyba.com.au/blogs/blog/the-science-behind-isometrics-building-stronger-tendons-for-athletes)
- **Add eccentric strengthening for the "braking" function.** Eccentric (slow-lowering) work trains muscles to decelerate and control movement into range — the exact capacity a hypermobile joint lacks. This is the backbone of the elbow-hypermobility protocols.
- **Progress tendon loading deliberately.** Tendons in hypermobile people can be prone to tendinopathy and slow to recover; follow the standard progression of isometrics → slow heavy isotonic/eccentric loading → energy-storage/speed work, and respect that tendons adapt more slowly than muscle.
- **Avoid static/developmental stretching of already-lax joints.** Climbing-specific and hypermobility sources agree: aggressive stretching of hypermobile joints tends to increase injury risk without benefit. Keep a small amount of muscle stiffness for control. (Stretching genuinely *tight* muscles elsewhere in the body is fine.)
- **Climbing-specific caveat on hypermobility.** The Climbing Doctor notes hypermobility can be an asset for reach and extreme positions but is associated with more joint dislocations and possibly more tendinopathy, and recommends learning "the ranges in which your joints are most stable and [trying] to remain within them," plus bracing/taping for hard-to-control joints (fingers, wrists), thorough warm-ups, and managing volume/recovery (hypermobile athletes may fatigue faster and recover slower).

### Elbow-specific: building end-range control without aggravating the joint

**A. Retrain "active lockout" (co-contraction) — the central drill for your mantle problem.**
- The core cue, from GMB Fitness for hyperextending elbows: find a neutral (not hyperextended) elbow position with your hand on the ground, then **co-contract biceps and triceps simultaneously and hold ~30 seconds**; progress by moving your body around while maintaining that active, muscular support. [GMB Fitness](https://gmb.io/hypermobility/) This is the closed-chain, weight-bearing version of learning muscular lockout and directly rehearses the mantle support position.
- The concept to internalize: replace "hang on the bone" with "hold with the muscle." In yoga-for-hypermobility teaching, the **micro-bend** is framed not as a rigid rule but as a *movement option* — can you support yourself at end range, slightly out of it, and move between the two on demand? Co-contraction is the tool that lets you do this.
- Jeannie Di Bon's staged approach: start non-weight-bearing (supine arm/shoulder mechanics) to learn control, build proprioception/awareness of the locking habit, strengthen the upper back so the elbow no longer needs to "prop" you, [jeanniedibon](https://jeanniedibon.com/what-to-do-with-hypermobile-elbow-joints/) then progress to loaded weight-bearing keeping the elbow supported rather than locked.

**B. Mid-range isometrics and eccentrics (open-chain, load-controlled).**
- **Isometric elbow holds** at ~90° and at partial extension (biceps and triceps against an immovable resistance) to build tolerance and position sense without joint travel.
- **Eccentric biceps "brake" work:** slowly lower a weight after a biceps curl, [BraceLab](https://bracelab.com/clinicians-classroom/treating-adolescent-elbow-hypermobility) emphasizing control as the elbow approaches (but does not reach) full extension — training the biceps to decelerate extension. [BraceLab](https://bracelab.com/clinicians-classroom/treating-adolescent-elbow-hypermobility) Keep within mid-range initially.
- **Single-arm skull-crushers / controlled tricep extensions** with a slow 3–4 s lowering tempo and a pause, stopping short of hyperextension, to build the triceps needed for mantling.
- **Weighted supination/pronation** (holding a hammer or light dumbbell) for forearm stability around the elbow.
- Progress toward standing single-arm cable/band tricep extensions and band biceps curls as control improves. Reps in these protocols are typically moderate (e.g., 2–4 sets of 6–10 for strength; higher reps for tolerance).

**C. Bracing/taping as a proprioceptive aid (optional, temporary).** A well-fitting elbow sleeve gives sensory feedback rather than mechanical restriction, useful during retraining or overnight if you passively hyperextend in sleep — but it's a tool alongside, not a substitute for, strengthening and awareness.

### Push-up alternatives and regressions for hypermobile elbows

The universal rule from every source: **never lock out into hyperextension at the top, and don't let the elbow snap/collapse.** Keep a soft micro-bend, keep muscles engaged, and control the range.

Regressions and alternatives, roughly easiest → hardest, that build the same pressing/triceps strength for mantling:
- **Wall push-ups** → **incline/box push-ups** (hands elevated on a bench/box; the higher the surface, the easier). [BigBeeFit](https://www.bigbeefit.com/blog/push-up-variations) Lower load and easier to keep form; stop short of full elbow extension at the top.
- **Kneeling push-ups** with a straight trunk and braced core, again stopping short of lockout.
- **Eccentric-only push-ups:** lower slowly (4–5 s) from the top in a plank, then reset on knees [BigBeeFit](https://www.bigbeefit.com/blog/push-up-variations) — builds pressing strength while training the controlled-lowering (braking) pattern.
- **Floor press (dumbbell or barbell):** the floor stops your upper arms at roughly the halfway point, *eliminating the deep bottom range* and, because you don't hit full straight-arm lockout under body weight, protecting the elbow end range. Excellent load-scalable pressing option.
- **Box/bench dips and straight-bar dips (partial range):** dips are among the most transferable to mantling ("straight-bar dips feel closer to pressing over a lip... move from support into lockout while the body travels around the hands"). [EVMT Brands](https://evmt.co/blogs/news/mantle-rock-climbing) [evmt](https://evmt.co/blogs/news/mantle-rock-climbing) Keep depth controlled and stop short of hyperextended lockout at the top; box dips let you regulate load and depth. Note dips demand shoulder mobility/health, so build tolerance gradually.
- **Bench support holds** (top-of-dip isometric): own the finished mantle position with active, co-contracted arms — directly addresses "mantles fall apart when climbers can't stabilize once partly over the edge." [EVMT Brands](https://evmt.co/blogs/news/mantle-rock-climbing)
- **Deficit push-ups on parallettes/handles:** train deeper shoulder flexion and pressing without the technical barrier of full dips, [EVMT Brands](https://evmt.co/blogs/news/mantle-rock-climbing) once elbows tolerate loading.
- **Cable/band chest press and close-grip press variations:** load pressing in a controlled arc without body-weight lockout.

For mantle transfer specifically, benchmarks cited in climbing coaching (for intermediate outdoor boulderers) are the ability to do controlled bodyweight dips before dynamic outdoor mantles; [Rock Climbing Realms](https://rockclimbingrealms.com/how-to-mantle-climbing/) treat these as aspirational targets to progress toward, not prerequisites, and always with a non-hyperextended lockout.

### Training the mantle itself as a skill

- **Break it into phases and rehearse low-consequence versions first.** On a box/bench/stacked pads: place both hands down, set one foot high, and slowly rehearse the pull-to-press transfer, pausing with weight partly over the support before standing (assisted box mantle; foot-switch mantle; palm-reposition drill). [evmt](https://evmt.co/blogs/news/mantle-rock-climbing)
- **Sequence cues:** "hips first, arms second"; "get high, then push"; "hip over foot"; "hands are posts" (turn the pressing hand into a post to press through, not a grip to pull on); "foot stays loaded." [evmt](https://evmt.co/blogs/news/mantle-rock-climbing) Drive the hip up and over the high foot *before* asking the triceps to finish, and let the shoulder travel forward over the palm without collapsing.
- **Deliberately keep an active elbow through the press.** Because your failure mode is hyperextension collapse, practice the stand-up with a muscularly controlled lockout (co-contraction) rather than snapping to a straight, passively locked arm. Finish continuously — hesitation half-way is a common cause of slips.
- **Progress:** floor drills → big gym volumes/friendly slab top-outs with big feet and broad hand surfaces → sloper/rounded-lip specificity → mantles added when fatigued (end of session) to build real-world robustness. [evmt](https://evmt.co/blogs/news/mantle-rock-climbing)

### Broader upper-body and shoulder-girdle work for hypermobile climbers

- **Scapular stability / posterior chain.** Building upper-back and scapular strength is repeatedly flagged as *the* foundation so the elbow (and shoulder) no longer over-compensate. Key exercises: scapular retraction holds (squeeze shoulder blades down and together ~10 s), [Advancerehab](https://advancerehab.com/boost-your-climbing-strength-decrease-the-likelihood-of-injury-and-improve-your-posture-all-at-once/) Blackburn "T"s and prone scapular work (mid/lower trapezius, rhomboids, serratus), bird-dog (core + scapular + hip control in a controlled range), and scapular push-ups/protractions for serratus.
- **Rotator cuff.** Shoulder instability commonly accompanies hypermobility, and climbers are prone to rotator-cuff issues. Train internal/external rotation with bands or light dumbbells (side-lying ER, "rocket ships"/"cheerleader" band drills that combine cuff + scapular work), emphasizing rotation with a stable scapula and elbow height held constant. Multiple short sessions favored.
- **Wrist stability (crucial for mantling/palming).** The Climbing Doctor documents wrist instability in hypermobile climbers using joint-position (laser-tracking) training and closed-chain weight-bearing drills; useful tools include banded wrist rock-backs to improve/support extension for mantles and push-ups, [The Climbing Doctor](https://theclimbingdoctor.com/enhancing-climbing-performance-unlocking-wrist-mobility-stability-and-strength/) reverse wrist curls and wide-pinch extension for the wrist stabilizers/extensors, and taping or a low-profile compression brace for support on slopers/mantles. [WeighMyRack](https://blog.weighmyrack.com/how-to-climb-with-heds/) Wrist mobility into extension is a prerequisite for comfortable weight-bearing in the mantle position.
- **Fingers/grip.** Ball squeezes and controlled grip work (keeping fingers in neutral, not hyperextended) improve grip control that is often lost when finger joints hyperextend.
- **Core and hips.** Bird-dog and hip mobility/turnout work (pigeon variations, seated external rotation, deep step-up isometric holds) support the mantle's rock-over phase; [evmt](https://evmt.co/blogs/news/mantle-rock-climbing) the Climbing Doctor notes hip range is a genuine performance asset for climbers but must be controlled.

## Recommendations

**Stage 0 — See a clinician first (and set a baseline).** Because you already have symptomatic elbow issues and can't currently do push-ups, get assessed by a physiotherapist — ideally one familiar with climbers or hypermobility — to rule out a specific tendon/ligament injury and confirm this is a control problem rather than acute damage. Every source stresses individualized programming for hypermobility.

**Stage 1 (roughly weeks 0–4) — Awareness, isometrics, and proprioception.**
- Learn co-contraction: practice the GMB "hand on the ground, co-contract biceps + triceps, hold 30 s" drill daily; progress to shifting your weight around while maintaining active support.
- Mid-range elbow isometrics (90° and partial extension) and closed-chain proprioceptive holds (quadruped/forearm and hands positions with a *supported*, not locked, elbow).
- Begin scapular retraction holds and light rotator-cuff band work; add banded wrist rock-backs for extension tolerance.
- Wall and high-incline push-ups stopped well short of lockout.
- *Threshold to progress:* pain-free co-contraction holds and incline push-ups with a controlled, non-hyperextending elbow.

**Stage 2 (roughly weeks 4–10) — Load the brake and the press.**
- Eccentric biceps lowers and slow tricep extensions/skull-crushers within a non-hyperextended range (3–4 s eccentric, pause).
- Progress pressing: lower the incline over time; add **floor presses** (naturally range-limited) and **box/bench dips** with controlled depth and active lockout.
- Continue scapular/cuff/wrist work; add bird-dog and hip-mobility drills.
- Begin floor-based mantle rehearsals (box mantle, foot-switch, palm reposition) with an actively controlled arm.
- *Threshold to progress:* controlled bench dips and floor presses without elbow pain or hyperextension; confident co-contracted support in the top-of-dip/bench-support position.

**Stage 3 (weeks 10+) — Climbing-specific pressing and mantle skill.**
- Straight-bar dips, deficit push-ups/parallettes, and (if tolerated) work toward controlled standard push-ups keeping a muscular micro-bend at the top.
- Progress mantling from big-footed gym slab top-outs to sloper/rounded-lip specificity, and add mantles at the end of sessions when fatigued.
- Keep taping/sleeve available for slopers and mantles as a proprioceptive aid.

**Benchmarks that would change the plan:**
- *Escalate/return to clinician* if you get sharp joint-line pain, swelling, clicking with pain, nerve symptoms (tingling in ring/little finger — possible ulnar involvement), [jeanniedibon](https://jeanniedibon.com/what-to-do-with-hypermobile-elbow-joints/) or any episode of the elbow giving way.
- *Progress load* when a given range is pain-free and you can maintain active lockout under control; *regress* if the elbow snaps back, aches after sessions, or you catch yourself defaulting to passive lockout.
- Adopt **supervised, progressively heavier** resistance training rather than only light bands/bodyweight — the Henriksen 2022 case series shows meaningful gains come from real, well-guided intensity, whereas the 2021 self-guided low-intensity RCT showed none.

## Caveats

- **General hypermobility ≠ EDS, but the exercise principles overlap.** Most cited evidence comes from joint hypermobility syndrome/hEDS or generalized/knee hypermobility populations; the elbow-specific and climbing-specific literature is thin, and several practical recommendations rest on expert clinical opinion (The Climbing Doctor, Jeannie Di Bon, GMB) and a small case report (Kaux 2013) rather than large trials.
- **Evidence is mixed on generic exercise.** The low-intensity self-guided RCT found no benefit over control, while supervised heavy training helped — so "exercise" is not automatically effective; dose, supervision, and individualization drive results. The heavy-training study (Henriksen 2022) was an uncontrolled case series in the knee, so causal certainty is limited and lower-limb findings may not fully transfer to the elbow.
- **The "always micro-bend / never lock out" advice is nuanced.** Some experts argue hypermobile people should learn to control and load their true end range too, not merely avoid it forever; the goal is *choice and control* over the range, not permanent avoidance. For you, given active symptoms, erring toward active-lockout/micro-bend while you build control is the prudent starting point.
- **Commercial and non-clinical sources.** Some cited pages are from businesses (gyms, chalk brands, brace makers) or fora; their exercise descriptions are broadly consistent with clinical sources but should be treated as practical illustration, not independent evidence. Individual response varies — progress by symptom response, not a fixed calendar.
- **Mantling carries fall risk.** Practice on pads with a spotter; a failed mantle sends you backward/sideways from a rotated position. [evmt](https://evmt.co/blogs/news/mantle-rock-climbing)
